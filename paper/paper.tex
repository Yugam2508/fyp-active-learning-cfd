\begin{abstract}
\noindent
Monte Carlo propagation of distributions (GUM Supplement~1) needs
$10^5$--$10^6$ evaluations of the measurement model. When the model is an
expensive simulation, it is replaced by a surrogate trained on a handful of
runs, and the surrogate's own error enters the uncertainty budget. We compare
surrogate strategies for a budget of twenty runs on four test models: a
benchmark from an earlier analysis whose dominant term matches one candidate
surrogate, a Gaussian plume model of an ammonia release, and the borehole and
Ishigami functions. Strategies cover single Gaussian processes (GPs) with five
kernel specifications and log-transformed variables, nine-member committees
of different surrogates combined uniformly or by cross-validation (CV) error
weights, and adaptive designs. Each is run with 30 paired seeds and scored on
the propagated mean, 95th percentile and exceedance probabilities against
reference Monte Carlo evaluations. Kernel specification changed the error up
to eighteen-fold: an amplitude hyperparameter held fixed produced an apparent
GP failure that the earlier analysis had attributed to domain size.
Logarithmic transformation of positive variables reduced the error up to
eightfold. A committee beat a well-specified GP only where one member matched
the response structure, and only with CV weighting; on the borehole model it
was 2.3 times worse. The adaptive designs tested gave no consistent gain, and
a spectral acquisition criterion was never better than plain variance.
Five-fold CV estimated the surrogate error conservatively, whereas the GP's
own predictive standard deviation understated it by up to 2.8 times where the
GP was weakest. We give practical rules for surrogate-based uncertainty
evaluation and a conservative way to include the surrogate contribution.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Evaluating measurement uncertainty by the propagation of distributions,
following Supplement~1 to the Guide to the Expression of Uncertainty in
Measurement (GUM~S1)~\cite{jcgm100,jcgm101}, requires the measurement model
$Y=f(\mathbf{X})$ to be evaluated once per Monte Carlo trial, typically
$10^5$ to $10^6$ times~\cite{cox2006,harris2014}. For many quantities of
practical interest $f$ is a numerical simulation. A computational fluid
dynamics (CFD) model of an accidental ammonia release~\cite{tan2017}, for
example, takes hours to days per run, and a practical budget is often fewer
than twenty runs.

The usual remedy is a \emph{surrogate} (emulator) trained on the available
runs and used in place of $f$~\cite{heidenreich2017,wang2022,ohagan2006}. Its
error is then a contribution to the uncertainty budget that must be evaluated,
not assumed negligible; Kennedy and O'Hagan~\cite{kennedy2001} treat such
model inadequacy as an explicit uncertainty source. Gaussian process (GP)
regression~\cite{sacks1989,rw2006} is the standard surrogate for computer
experiments. A widely used guideline asks for about ten runs per
input~\cite{loeppky2009}, and GP results are known to depend on modelling
choices that are easy to get wrong~\cite{chen2016}. For smaller budgets,
alternatives include weighted committees of different
surrogates~\cite{goel2007,viana2009}, adaptive selection of runs where the
surrogate is uncertain~\cite{gramacy2009}, and, recently, spectral measures of
disagreement~\cite{lin2024}.

An earlier analysis by the first author combined these ideas: a heterogeneous
committee, a spectral acquisition criterion (UEigV) and dynamic model
management. It reported that a passively sampled GP fails catastrophically on
large input domains with a high output dynamic range, and that the committee
recovers accuracy at the same budget. This paper re-examines those claims with
correctly specified baselines and extends the comparison to models where no
committee member matches the response. Its contributions are:
\begin{enumerate}\setlength\itemsep{1pt}
\item a controlled, paired comparison of single-GP, committee and adaptive
strategies at a budget of twenty runs on four models, scored on the
quantities a GUM~S1 evaluation reports;
\item the finding that the reported GP failure is a kernel-specification
artefact, and that kernel specification and variable transformation matter
more than the choice of strategy;
\item a characterisation of when a committee helps (a member matching the
dominant structure, combined by error weights) and when adaptive designs help
(inconsistently, for the criteria tested);
\item an assessment of how far the surrogate's error can be estimated from the
training runs alone, and a conservative way to include it in a GUM~S1
evaluation.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Problem and protocol}
\subsection{Surrogate-based propagation}
Given input distributions $g_{\mathbf X}$ and $N$ runs
$\{(\mathbf{x}_i,f(\mathbf{x}_i))\}$, a surrogate $\hat f$ is fitted and the
Monte Carlo method of GUM~S1 is applied to $\hat f$ instead of $f$. Here
$N=20$, and the propagated mean, standard deviation (SD), 95th percentile
$y_{0.95}$ and exceedance probabilities are compared with those obtained from
$f$ itself. All input distributions are uniform over the ranges given below.

\subsection{Test models}
\emph{Physics} is the benchmark of the earlier analysis,
$f(\mathbf{x})=\sin 2x_1\cos 2x_2+\mathrm{e}^{-x_3^2}+0.2\lVert\mathbf{x}\rVert^2$
on $[-h,h]^3$ with $h=200$ unless stated, observed with noise of SD 0.1. Its
quadratic term carries 44\% of the output variance at $h=2$ and practically
all of it for $h\ge10$, and lies exactly in the class of a degree-2 polynomial
chaos expansion.

\emph{Plume} is the ground-level concentration of a continuous release from a
Gaussian plume model with ground reflection,
\begin{equation}
C=\frac{10^3\,Q}{2\uppi\,u\,\sigma_y\sigma_z}
\exp\!\left(-\frac{y^2}{2\sigma_y^2}\right)
2\exp\!\left(-\frac{H^2}{2\sigma_z^2}\right),
\end{equation}
in mg\,m$^{-3}$, with Briggs rural dispersion coefficients for neutral
stability, $\sigma_y=0.08x(1+10^{-4}x)^{-1/2}$ and
$\sigma_z=0.06x(1+1.5\times10^{-3}x)^{-1/2}$. Inputs: emission rate
$Q\in[100,2000]$\,g\,s$^{-1}$, wind speed $u\in[1,6]$\,m\,s$^{-1}$, release
height $H\in[1,20]$\,m, downwind distance $x\in[200,1000]$\,m and crosswind
offset $y\in[-30,30]$\,m. The output spans 1.8 to 2400\,mg\,m$^{-3}$, and the
probability of exceeding the level immediately dangerous to life or health
(IDLH) for ammonia, 210\,mg\,m$^{-3}$ (about 300\,ppm), is 0.123. The plume
model is an analytical proxy for dispersion problems, cheap enough for exact
reference values; it is not a CFD simulation.

\emph{Borehole} (water flow through a borehole, eight inputs, standard ranges)
and \emph{Ishigami} ($\sin x_1+7\sin^2x_2+0.1x_3^4\sin x_1$ on
$[-\uppi,\uppi]^3$) are standard test functions for computer experiments. No
candidate surrogate matches the plume, borehole or Ishigami response.

\subsection{Strategies}
\paragraph{Single GPs.} Each is fitted to a 20-point Latin hypercube. Five
kernel specifications are compared: (i) the original kernel of the earlier
analysis, a squared exponential with one length scale and an amplitude that
is \emph{fixed} at 1 on the standardised output rather than estimated; (ii)
with the amplitude estimated; (iii) with one length scale per input
(automatic relevance determination, ARD) but the amplitude fixed; (iv) with
both; (v) a Mat\'ern-5/2 ARD kernel with amplitude. Hyperparameters are
estimated by maximising the log marginal likelihood (L-BFGS-B, five random
restarts) on standardised inputs and outputs, with length scales in
$[10^{-2},10^3]$, amplitude in $[10^{-3},10^3]$ and a nugget variance in
$[10^{-8},10^{-1}]$. A sixth variant fits (v) to $\log Y$, with logarithmic
transformation of inputs whose range is strictly positive (plume and
borehole); predictions are back-transformed as $\exp$ of the latent mean,
i.e.\ the predictive median, without bias correction. The adaptive GP starts
from five Latin hypercube runs and adds 15 at its largest posterior SD over a
random candidate pool~\cite{gramacy2009}.

\paragraph{Committees.} Nine members: two GPs (squared exponential and
Mat\'ern-3/2, with amplitude and ARD), polynomial chaos expansions
(PCE)~\cite{xiu2002,sudret2008} of degrees 2 and 3 (Bayesian ridge
regression), random forests of 50 and 100 trees, a support vector regressor
and two neural networks (two hidden layers of 64 and 128 units). Members are
combined uniformly or with weights
\begin{equation}
w_k\propto\left[\mathrm{MSE}_{\mathrm{CV},k}\,(1+\pi_k)\right]^{-1},
\label{eq:w}
\end{equation}
where $\mathrm{MSE}_{\mathrm{CV},k}$ is the three-fold cross-validation error
of member $k$ (a single 80/20 hold-out for the networks) and $\pi_k\ge0$
penalises predictions below a known physical bound~\cite{goel2007,viana2009}.
A committee with the original GP kernel is included for reference. The
passive committee is fitted to the same 20-point design as the single GPs.
Adaptive committees start from five runs and add 15 where the active members
disagree most, scored by the variance of their predictions or by UEigV.

\paragraph{UEigV.} Lin et al.~\cite{lin2024} proposed this measure for
black-box language models, from the normalised graph Laplacian of pairwise
similarities between sampled outputs. Here the graph is built from the $K$
standardised member predictions $\hat y_k$ at a candidate input, with
$A_{ij}=\exp(-(\hat y_i-\hat y_j)^2/2)$,
$\mathbf L=\mathbf I-\mathbf D^{-1/2}\mathbf A\mathbf D^{-1/2}$ and
$\mathrm{UEigV}=\sum_k\max(0,1-\lambda_k)$, a continuous count of agreement
clusters.

\subsection{Evaluation}
Each strategy is run with seeds 1000--1029. Seeds are shared and the Latin
hypercube is seeded from the global random state, so passive strategies use
the identical design and adaptive ones the identical start. Accuracy over the
input domain is the root mean square error (RMSE) on 5000 test points divided
by the output SD (normalised RMSE; 1 corresponds to a constant predictor).
The surrogate propagates $3\times10^4$ input draws; the reference uses
$2\times10^5$ draws of $f$. Two exceedance assessments are distinguished:
$P(Y>y_{0.95})$ at the reference 95th percentile, whose true value is 0.05 by
construction, tests tail accuracy on every model; $P(C>\mathrm{IDLH})$ at a
fixed physical threshold is the quantity a risk decision uses. Strategies are
compared by two-sided Wilcoxon signed-rank tests on paired $\log$ normalised
RMSE. The 36 tests are treated as exploratory and their $p$-values are
Holm-adjusted. All errors above are \emph{validation} errors, which need the
reference model; Section~\ref{sec:estimate} examines what can be estimated
without it.

\section{Results}
\subsection{Kernel specification and variable transformation}
Table~\ref{tab:nrmse} lists every strategy on every model and
Table~\ref{tab:tests} the paired comparisons. On the physics model the
original kernel reaches a normalised RMSE of 0.59; estimating the amplitude
alone reduces it to 0.033, an eighteen-fold improvement in all 30 seeds
(adjusted $p<10^{-6}$). ARD alone does not help (0.61). With the amplitude
fixed at the sample variance of the standardised output, the GP cannot
represent a quadratic bowl whose values far exceed one SD and reverts towards
the mean between the runs.

Fig.~\ref{fig:dom} repeats this over the domain sweep of the earlier analysis.
With the original kernel the normalised RMSE is 0.60 at every half-width from
10 to 200, which the earlier analysis read as a failure of GPs on large
domains. A Mat\'ern-5/2 ARD GP with amplitude reaches 0.17 at $h=10$ and 0.08
from 50 to 200. The committee of the earlier analysis remains more accurate,
falling to $1.7\times10^{-4}$ at $h=200$, but because its degree-2 PCE
represents the quadratic term exactly: its median RMSE stays at 0.62--0.68,
close to the RMS of the oscillatory and exponential terms (0.50--0.61) that no
member can learn from twenty runs.

On the borehole model ARD is what matters (0.30 to 0.09), and on the plume and
Ishigami models no kernel specification helps. Logarithmic transformation
gives the largest single improvement where it applies: 0.089 to 0.011 on the
borehole model (eightfold, all 30 seeds) and 0.60 to 0.42 on the plume (27 of
30 seeds). No strategy does better than 0.89 on the Ishigami function, for
which the ten-runs-per-input guideline~\cite{loeppky2009} would ask for 30
runs.

\subsection{Committees, aggregation and adaptive runs}
Fig.~\ref{fig:strategies} summarises all strategies. A committee outperforms
the best single GP only on the physics model, by more than two orders of
magnitude, and only through its degree-2 PCE, on which the CV weights
concentrate. On the borehole model the CV-weighted committee is 2.3 times
worse than the Mat\'ern-5/2 GP on the identical design (0 of 30 seeds better,
adjusted $p<10^{-6}$); on the plume and Ishigami models the two tie.

Aggregation matters wherever a committee helps. Uniform averaging is worse
than CV weighting on the physics model by a factor of about 2000 and on the
borehole model by about 20\% (adjusted $p\le0.002$), with either GP specification,
and is never better. On the physics model a prior-reverted or otherwise poor
member is enough to move a uniform mean by orders of magnitude even when an
exact member is present.

Within the designs tested (five initial runs; 15 adaptive runs chosen by GP
posterior SD, committee variance or UEigV over a random candidate pool),
adaptive selection gave no consistent benefit. For the single GP it was never
significantly better and was worse on the plume before adjustment. For the
committee it helped on the physics and borehole models (median ratios 0.80
and 0.61), hurt on the plume (1.25, adjusted $p=0.005$; median error of the
propagated mean 55\%) and made no difference on the Ishigami function. UEigV
was never better than variance acquisition after adjustment and was 1.7
times worse on the borehole model (adjusted $p<10^{-6}$). Criteria aimed at a
particular quantile or threshold~\cite{gramacy2009} may behave differently.

\subsection{Estimating the surrogate contribution}
\label{sec:estimate}
In practice the reference is unavailable and the surrogate's contribution
must be estimated from the training runs. Table~\ref{tab:est} compares, for
the recommended GPs, the true error with two estimates available in practice:
the five-fold CV RMSE with hyperparameters re-fitted in every fold, and the
root mean square of the GP's own predictive SD over the domain.

CV was conservative. It was at least the true error in 28 and 30 of 30 seeds
on the physics and borehole models, where the GP is accurate and the
held-out folds are relatively harder, close to it on the plume and Ishigami
models, and below half the true error in at most 6 of 30 seeds (plume, log
variables). The GP's own uncertainty was unreliable in the unsafe direction
where the GP is weakest: on the plume and Ishigami models it understated the
error 1.7--2.8 times and the nominal 95\% predictive intervals covered
73--77\% of test points; on the physics model and the log-variable borehole GP
it overstated the error. The member envelope of a committee, read as an
interval for $P(Y>y_{0.95})$~\cite{ferson1996}, contained the reference value
in 0 to 100\% of seeds depending on model and strategy, and is not a
substitute.

These results suggest a simple, conservative treatment within GUM~S1:
propagate $Y=\hat f(\mathbf X)+E_{\mathrm s}$, where the surrogate error
$E_{\mathrm s}$ is an additional input with expectation zero and standard
uncertainty equal to the CV RMSE, independent of $\mathbf X$. It ignores the
spatial variation and correlation of the surrogate error and inflates the
uncertainty where the surrogate is accurate. Calibrated alternatives, for
example conformal prediction~\cite{angelopoulos2023}, need validation on a
CFD case.

\section{Relation to the earlier analysis}
The earlier analysis made five claims. Three are withdrawn or revised. The
catastrophic GP failure on large domains is a kernel-specification artefact
(Section~3.1). The spectral acquisition criterion gives no advantage over
plain committee variance; on the physics model the earlier paired campaign
already favoured variance ($p=0.047$, 30 versus 27 of 30 successes), and
here UEigV is never better. The committee's advantage is not general but
comes from a member matching the dominant term. Two claims survive and are
strengthened: aggregation must be error-weighted, and hard rejection of
members is a degenerate baseline (in the earlier campaign it collapsed to one
member in all 30 trials and then sampled at random for 31\% of the budget).
The records of those campaigns are kept in the repository~\cite{repo}.

\section{Discussion and limitations}
The study is deliberately narrow: four analytical models, uniform inputs, one
budget ($N=20$), one design size for adaptive starts, and acquisition by
variance-type criteria. The plume model represents the structure of a
dispersion problem but not the irregularities of a CFD solution, such as
recirculation, regime changes or numerical noise; a CFD case is the
necessary next step. Results at larger budgets may differ, in particular for
adaptive designs. The paired tests are exploratory and Holm-adjusted; several
nominal differences (uniform versus CV weighting on the plume, adaptive
versus passive GP on the plume, UEigV versus variance on three models) do not
survive the adjustment and are not claimed. The log transformation applies
only to positive outputs, and its back-transformation to the predictive
median slightly underestimates the mean of a skewed output. Finally, the
committee's components and hyperparameters were not tuned per model, which
reflects practice at this budget but may understate what a tuned committee
could achieve.

\section{Practical rules}
For surrogate-based GUM~S1 evaluations with about twenty runs:
\begin{enumerate}\setlength\itemsep{1pt}
\item \emph{Specify the GP fully}: estimate an amplitude and one length scale
per input; inspect the fitted hyperparameters.
\item \emph{Transform the variables} when the model is multiplicative or
positive, for example concentrations and flows.
\item \emph{Use a committee only with error weighting}, and expect a gain only
when a member can represent the dominant structure.
\item \emph{Prefer a space-filling design} unless an adaptive criterion has
been shown to help for the quantity of interest.
\item \emph{Estimate the surrogate contribution by cross-validation} and
include it in the budget; do not rely on the GP's own predictive variance or
on committee spread.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Conclusion}
For Monte Carlo uncertainty propagation with twenty runs of an expensive
model, the kernel specification of a GP surrogate and the transformation of
the variables mattered more than the choice between single surrogates,
committees and the adaptive designs tested. A GP failure reported in an
earlier analysis was caused by one hyperparameter held fixed. Committees
helped only when a member matched the response structure and only with CV
error weighting. Cross-validation gave a conservative estimate of the
surrogate's error; the GP's own uncertainty did not. A CFD dispersion case,
other budgets and a calibrated surrogate contribution are the next steps.
