\begin{abstract}
\noindent\textit{Abstract} -- The Monte Carlo method of GUM Supplement~1
needs $10^5$--$10^6$ evaluations of the measurement model, which is infeasible
when the model is a computational fluid dynamics (CFD) simulation costing
hours per run. Replacing the model by a surrogate trained on about twenty runs
makes propagation cheap, but it adds a model-form uncertainty component that
the surrogate itself does not report. We show that the standard surrogate, a
Gaussian process, fails catastrophically only when a large input domain
coincides with a high output dynamic range. In that regime, a committee of
structurally different surrogates recovers accuracy at the same budget. Paired
30-seed ablations, however, show that the cause is not the spectral
disagreement metric used to select runs. Two things are responsible: one
committee member matching the dominant response structure, and an
error-weighted aggregation rule. Uniform averaging fails in every trial. The
weighted committee then supports a probability box, bounded exceedance
probabilities and Sobol indices with model-form uncertainty bars, and it
recovers a ground-truth Monte Carlo reference to within about 1\,\%.

\smallskip\noindent\textit{Keywords} -- measurement uncertainty; GUM
Supplement~1; surrogate model; active learning; model-form uncertainty
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Evaluating measurement uncertainty by the propagation of distributions
(GUM~S1~\cite{jcgm2008}) requires the measurement model $Y=f(\mathbf{X})$ to
be evaluated for every Monte Carlo trial. For many quantities of practical
interest, $f$ is itself a numerical simulation. One example is the
concentration downstream of an accidental ammonia release in an industrial
hall~\cite{tan2017}: one CFD run takes hours to days, while the standard error
of a Monte Carlo estimate falls only as $M^{-1/2}$. A practical budget is
often fewer than twenty runs.

The usual remedy is a \emph{surrogate} (emulator) trained on those runs and
used in place of $f$ inside the Monte Carlo loop~\cite{heidenreich2017,
wang2022}. This trades an intractable cost for a new contribution to the
uncertainty budget: the error of the surrogate itself. From a metrological
point of view, this term must be evaluated and reported, not assumed away.
The workhorse surrogate, Gaussian-process (GP) regression~\cite{sacks1989,
rw2006}, provides a predictive variance, but that variance is conditional on
the GP being an adequate model. Where it is not, the GP is confidently
wrong~\cite{huellermeier2021}.

This contribution asks two questions relevant to TC21. (i)~When does a
passively sampled GP surrogate fail as a stand-in for an expensive measurement
model? (ii)~When it fails, which ingredients of an adaptive multi-surrogate
scheme actually restore a trustworthy propagation, with the surrogate
(model-form) uncertainty quantified?

\section{Proposed Contribution}
\subsection{Failure criterion for passive GP surrogates}
The GP posterior mean is $\hat f(\mathbf{x})=\mu+\mathbf{k}_\ast^{\top}
(\mathbf{K}+\sigma_\varepsilon^2\mathbf{I})^{-1}(\mathbf{y}-\mu\mathbf{1})$.
When the distance from $\mathbf{x}$ to every training point greatly exceeds
the length scale, $\mathbf{k}_\ast\!\to\!\mathbf{0}$ and $\hat f\!\to\!\mu$.
We call this \emph{prior reversion}. We show empirically that it is harmful
only when two conditions hold together: the input domain is large relative to
the sample density, \emph{and} the output dynamic range is high, so that the
prior mean is far from the response. Domain size alone is not sufficient.

\subsection{Committee scheme under study}
A committee of $K{=}9$ surrogates with different inductive biases (two GPs,
polynomial chaos expansions (PCE) of degrees 2 and~3, two random forests, a
support-vector regressor and two neural networks) is trained on a 5-point
Latin-hypercube design. Fifteen further runs are chosen sequentially where
the committee disagrees most. Disagreement is scored either by a spectral
measure (UEigV, the eigenvalue sum of the normalised graph Laplacian of the
$K$ predictions~\cite{lin2024}) or by the plain committee variance. Two
\emph{model managers} combine the members. The first is hard rejection, which
removes inconsistent members and averages the survivors uniformly. The second
weights the members by inverse cross-validation error,
\begin{equation}
w_k\propto\bigl[\mathrm{CV\text{-}MSE}_k\,(1+\pi_k)\bigr]^{-1},
\label{eq:w}
\end{equation}
where $\pi_k\!\ge\!0$ optionally penalises violations of known physical
bounds. Eq.~\eqref{eq:w} is the weighted-average surrogate
of~\cite{goel2007,viana2009}. We do not propose it as new. We characterise
when it is \emph{necessary}.

\begin{algorithm}[t]
\caption{Adaptive committee surrogate for propagation}
\label{alg:al}
\small
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require input PDF $g_{\mathbf X}$, budget $N$, $N_0{=}5$, acquisition $a$
\State $\mathcal D\gets$ Latin hypercube of $N_0$ runs of $f$; fit all members
\For{$t=1,\dots,N-N_0$}
  \State $\mathbf x^\ast\gets\arg\max a(\mathbf x)$ over a random candidate pool
  \State run $f(\mathbf x^\ast)$; add it to $\mathcal D$; refit members
  \State update weights $w_k$ (Eq.~\ref{eq:w}) or reject members
\EndFor
\State draw $\mathbf X_m\sim g_{\mathbf X}$, $m=1..M$; evaluate every member
\State \Return $\mu_w$, $\sigma^2_{\mathrm{mf}}$, p-box, exceedance bounds, Sobol
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\subsection{Propagation with a model-form component}
The trained committee replaces $f$ in a GUM~S1 Monte Carlo loop
($5\times10^4$ trials, seconds of computing time). Following the law of total
variance,
\begin{equation}
\operatorname{Var}[Y]=\operatorname{Var}_{\mathbf{X}}\!\bigl[\mu_w(\mathbf{X})\bigr]
+\mathrm{E}_{\mathbf{X}}\!\bigl[\sigma^2_{\mathrm{mf}}(\mathbf{X})\bigr],
\label{eq:lotv}
\end{equation}
the output variance splits into an input-driven part (the weighted committee
mean $\mu_w$) and a model-form part $\sigma^2_{\mathrm{mf}}$ (the weighted
spread between members). The ratio of the second term to the total is a
stopping diagnostic: further simulator runs are worthwhile while it is large.
The members' individual output CDFs form a probability box~\cite{ferson1996},
from which exceedance probabilities $P(Y>y_{\mathrm c})$ are reported as
intervals. Sobol indices~\cite{saltelli2010,jansen1999} are computed per
member, which yields bars on the sensitivity ranking.

\section{Preliminary Results}
\subsection{Protocol}
All results use the test function
$f(\mathbf{x})=\sin 2x_1\cos 2x_2+e^{-x_3^2}+0.2\lVert\mathbf{x}\rVert^2$ on
$[-200,200]^3$ with a budget of $N{=}20$ runs. Each arm uses 30 paired seeds
(the same seed gives every arm the same initial design). RMSE is evaluated on
one fixed 5000-point test set. Arms are compared by a Wilcoxon signed-rank test
on log-RMSE. A trial counts as a success if RMSE${}<200$, an order of
magnitude below the ${\approx}2000$ of a prior-reverted GP.

\subsection{Failure criterion}
A domain-size sweep with half-widths $h\in\{2,10,50,100,200\}$ shows a sharp
crossover between $h{=}2$ (ratio of adaptive to passive RMSE $\rho{=}1.06$)
and $h{=}10$ ($\rho{=}0.38$). The Schwefel function on the larger domain
$[-500,500]^3$ but with a small dynamic range gives $\rho{=}1.09$: passive
GP sampling is adequate there. Across nine standard benchmarks, only the
problem that meets both conditions favours the adaptive scheme
(Fig.~\ref{fig:dom}). On seven of the nine, a well-specified passive GP ties
or wins by 9--17\,\%.

Three findings follow. \emph{First}, the spectral metric gives no benefit:
plain variance succeeds in all 30 trials and is better on paired RMSE in
18 of 30 ($p{=}0.047$). \emph{Second}, success depends on an \emph{anchor}
member. Without the PCE members, the scheme fails in every trial, as does
the conventional single-GP adaptive design. With them, hard rejection ends
with PCE of degree~2 alone in 26 of 30 trials. That PCE is the exact model
class of the dominant quadratic term, which carries more than 99.9\,\% of the
output variance. \emph{Third}, hard rejection collapses to one member in all
30 trials, at a median of iteration 10 of 15. After that, disagreement is
identically zero and about 31\,\% of its run budget is silently spent on random
points.

Table~\ref{tab:agg} isolates the aggregation rule. With three survivors that
include the correct member, a uniform mean fails in every trial, because the
prior-reverted GP's error of order $10^3$ dominates it. The same three members
weighted by Eq.~\eqref{eq:w} succeed in every trial. Making rejection reversible
has no measurable effect. The aggregation rule is the causal ingredient.

The central estimates of the two managers are indistinguishable. The
difference is entirely in whether the model-form component can be evaluated.

With the weighted committee, the median relative error of the propagated
output median is 0.24\,\% over 30 trials. A representative run agrees with a
$2\times10^5$-trial ground-truth Monte Carlo to within 1\,\% in mean, standard
deviation and the 5th, 50th and 95th percentiles. In that run, the model-form
fraction of Eq.~\eqref{eq:lotv} falls from 0.76 to almost zero over the
budget. The model-form intervals are committee envelopes rather than
calibrated coverage intervals, and they under-cover. Over 30 trials, the
interval for the mean contains the true mean in 97\,\% of trials. The
probability box contains on average 68\,\% of the true CDF. The interval for
$P(Y>y_{0.95})$ contains the truth in 60\,\% of trials. A single-member
committee from hard rejection gives zero-width intervals by construction, so
its model-form component is not evaluable at all.

\section{Implications for Uncertainty Evaluation}
The results suggest four practical rules for anyone using a surrogate inside a
GUM~S1 evaluation of an expensive model.

\textit{Check the two failure conditions before trusting a GP.} If the input
ranges are wide compared with the spacing of the available runs, and the
output spans orders of magnitude, a passively sampled GP will reproduce its
prior mean over most of the domain. Its own predictive variance will not
reveal this. Where either condition is absent, a GP fitted to a
space-filling design was as good as any adaptive scheme in our tests.

\textit{Report the surrogate as an uncertainty component.} Eq.~\eqref{eq:lotv}
gives a variance share for the surrogate that can be entered in the
uncertainty budget next to the input contributions. A single surrogate, or a
committee reduced to one member, sets this share to zero by construction.
That is an omission, not a demonstration of accuracy.

\textit{Weight members by out-of-sample error, never uniformly.} A committee
mean is only as good as its weights. A single prior-reverted member is enough
to move a uniform mean by orders of magnitude, even when an exact member is
present. Cross-validation error is cheap at $N\approx20$, and it separates
good and bad members by orders of magnitude, which is all Eq.~\eqref{eq:w}
needs.

\textit{Treat committee envelopes as lower bounds on model-form uncertainty.}
The intervals in Table~\ref{tab:uq} contain the true exceedance probability
in only about 60\,\% of trials. They show \emph{that} the surrogate is
uncertain, not with what probability. Calibrated coverage needs a further
step, such as conformal prediction, which the full paper will evaluate.

\section{Conclusions}
For an expensive measurement model evaluated about twenty times, a passively
sampled GP surrogate is safe for Monte Carlo propagation unless the input
domain is large and the output dynamic range is high. When both hold, a
heterogeneous committee restores accuracy. The reason, however, is a member
matching the dominant response structure, combined with inverse
cross-validation-error weighting. A sophisticated run-selection metric is not
responsible, and uniform averaging fails even with the right member present.
The weighted committee also makes the surrogate's own model-form contribution
visible in the uncertainty budget, as a probability box and a variance share.
These results come from a single synthetic model in which an anchor member is
available. The full paper will add test cases with no such member and a
dispersion-type model. It will also replace the uncalibrated envelopes with
conformal intervals~\cite{angelopoulos2023}, which have guaranteed coverage.
Code and every per-trial record are openly available at
\url{https://github.com/Yugam2508/fyp-active-learning-cfd}.
