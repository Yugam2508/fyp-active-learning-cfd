\title{Surrogate-based Monte Carlo propagation for expensive measurement
models: when Gaussian processes fail and why committee aggregation must be
error-weighted}

\author[a1]{Vora Yugam Jinesh}
\affiliation[a1]{Department of Engineering Science, National University of
Singapore, Singapore}
\CorrespondingAuthorNumber{1}
\CorrespondingAuthorEmail{yjv2508@gmail.com}
\funding{}

\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
Monte Carlo propagation of distributions needs thousands of evaluations of the
measurement model, which is infeasible when the model is a fluid dynamics
simulation costing hours per run. A surrogate trained on about twenty runs
makes propagation cheap, but it adds a model-form uncertainty component that
is rarely evaluated. We show that a passively sampled Gaussian process fails
catastrophically only when a large input domain coincides with a high output
dynamic range. In that regime, a committee of structurally different
surrogates restores accuracy. Paired 30-seed ablations show, however, that the
run-selection metric is not the cause. The decisive ingredients are a member
matching the dominant response structure and error-weighted aggregation;
uniform averaging fails in every trial. The weighted committee yields a
probability box, bounded exceedance probabilities and a model-form variance
share, and it reproduces a reference Monte Carlo evaluation to within about
\qty{1}{\percent}.
\end{abstract}

\begin{keywords}
Measurement uncertainty; Monte Carlo method; Surrogate model; Active learning;
Model-form uncertainty
\end{keywords}

\section{Introduction}
Evaluating measurement uncertainty by the propagation of distributions,
following Supplement~1 to the Guide to the Expression of Uncertainty in
Measurement (GUM~S1)~\cite{jcgm100,jcgm101}, requires the measurement model
$Y=f(\mathbf{X})$ to be evaluated once per Monte Carlo trial. For many
quantities of practical interest, $f$ is itself a numerical simulation. One
example is the concentration downstream of an accidental ammonia release in an
industrial hall~\cite{tan2017}. A single computational fluid dynamics (CFD) run
of such a release takes hours to days, while the standard error of a Monte
Carlo estimate falls only as $M^{-1/2}$ in the number of trials $M$. A
practical budget is often fewer than twenty runs.

The usual remedy is a \emph{surrogate} (emulator) trained on those runs and
used in place of $f$ inside the Monte Carlo loop~\cite{heidenreich2017,
wang2022}. This trades an intractable cost for a new contribution to the
uncertainty budget: the error of the surrogate itself. From a metrological
point of view, this contribution must be evaluated and reported, not assumed
to be negligible. The standard surrogate, Gaussian process (GP)
regression~\cite{sacks1989,rw2006}, provides a predictive variance, but that
variance is conditional on the GP being an adequate model. Where it is not,
the GP is confidently wrong~\cite{huellermeier2021}.

Committee-based schemes respond by training several structurally different
surrogates, choosing new runs where they disagree, and combining their
predictions. Weighted combinations of surrogates are established in design
optimisation~\cite{goel2007,viana2009}, and spectral measures of disagreement
have recently been proposed for uncertainty quantification of language
models~\cite{lin2024}. Their behaviour as tools for uncertainty
\emph{evaluation} of expensive measurement models has not been dissected.

This contribution addresses two questions. First, when does a passively
sampled GP surrogate fail as a stand-in for an expensive measurement model?
Second, when it fails, which ingredients of an adaptive multi-surrogate scheme
actually restore a trustworthy propagation, with the surrogate (model-form)
contribution quantified?

\section{Proposed contribution}
\subsection{Failure criterion for passive GP surrogates}
For a GP with prior mean $\mu$, the posterior mean at an input $\mathbf{x}$ is
\begin{equation}
\hat f(\mathbf{x})=\mu+\mathbf{k}_\ast^{\top}
\left(\mathbf{K}+\sigma_\varepsilon^2\mathbf{I}\right)^{-1}
(\mathbf{y}-\mu\mathbf{1}),
\label{eq:gp}
\end{equation}
where $\mathbf{K}$ is the covariance matrix of the training inputs,
$\mathbf{k}_\ast$ the covariance between $\mathbf{x}$ and the training inputs,
$\mathbf{y}$ the training outputs and $\sigma_\varepsilon^2$ the noise
variance. When the distance from $\mathbf{x}$ to every training point greatly
exceeds the kernel length scale, $\mathbf{k}_\ast\to\mathbf{0}$ and
$\hat f\to\mu$. We call this \emph{prior reversion}. We show empirically that
it is harmful only when two conditions hold together: the input domain is
large compared with the spacing of the runs, \emph{and} the output dynamic
range is high, so that the prior mean is far from the response. Domain size
alone is not sufficient.

\subsection{Committee scheme under study}
A committee of $K=9$ surrogates with different inductive biases is used: two
GPs, polynomial chaos expansions (PCE) of degrees 2 and 3, two random forests,
a support vector regressor and two neural networks. The committee is trained on
a five-point Latin hypercube design, and fifteen further runs are then chosen
sequentially where the members disagree most (Algorithm~\ref{alg:al}).
Disagreement is scored either by a spectral measure (UEigV, the eigenvalue
sum of the normalised graph Laplacian built from the $K$
predictions~\cite{lin2024}) or by the plain committee variance.

Two \emph{model managers} combine the members. Hard rejection removes members
that are inconsistent with the committee and averages the survivors uniformly.
Error weighting assigns
\begin{equation}
w_k\propto\left[\mathrm{MSE}_{\mathrm{CV},k}\,(1+\pi_k)\right]^{-1},
\label{eq:w}
\end{equation}
where $\mathrm{MSE}_{\mathrm{CV},k}$ is the cross-validation mean squared
error of member $k$ and $\pi_k\ge0$ optionally penalises violations of known
physical bounds, such as a non-negative concentration. Eq.~\eqref{eq:w} is the
weighted-average surrogate of~\cite{goel2007,viana2009}. We do not propose it
as new; we characterise when it is \emph{necessary}.

\begin{algorithm}[tb]
\caption{Adaptive committee surrogate for propagation}
\label{alg:al}
\small
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require input PDF $g_{\mathbf X}$, budget $N$, $N_0=5$, acquisition $a$
\State $\mathcal D\gets$ Latin hypercube of $N_0$ runs of $f$; fit all members
\For{$t=1,\dots,N-N_0$}
  \State $\mathbf x^\ast\gets\arg\max a(\mathbf x)$ over a random candidate pool
  \State run $f(\mathbf x^\ast)$, add it to $\mathcal D$, refit the members
  \State update weights by Eq.~\eqref{eq:w}, or reject members
\EndFor
\State draw $\mathbf X_m\sim g_{\mathbf X}$, $m=1,\dots,M$; evaluate all members
\State \Return $\mu_w$, $\sigma^2_{\mathrm{mf}}$, p-box, exceedance bounds
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\subsection{Propagation with a model-form component}
The trained committee replaces $f$ in a GUM~S1 Monte Carlo evaluation with
$M=\num{5e4}$ trials, which takes seconds of computing time. By the law of
total variance,
\begin{equation}
\operatorname{Var}[Y]=\operatorname{Var}_{\mathbf{X}}\left[\mu_w(\mathbf{X})\right]
+\mathrm{E}_{\mathbf{X}}\left[\sigma^2_{\mathrm{mf}}(\mathbf{X})\right],
\label{eq:lotv}
\end{equation}
the output variance splits into an input-driven part, carried by the weighted
committee mean $\mu_w$, and a model-form part $\sigma^2_{\mathrm{mf}}$, the
weighted spread between members. The ratio of the second term to the total is
a stopping diagnostic: further simulator runs are worthwhile while it is
large. The members' individual output cumulative distribution functions
(CDFs) form a probability box (p-box)~\cite{ferson1996}, from which an
exceedance probability $P(Y>y_{\mathrm c})$ is reported as an interval. Sobol
indices~\cite{saltelli2010,jansen1999} computed per member give bars on the
sensitivity ranking.

\section{Preliminary results and potential applications}
\subsection{Protocol}
All results use the test model
$f(\mathbf{x})=\sin 2x_1\cos 2x_2+\mathrm{e}^{-x_3^2}+0.2\lVert\mathbf{x}\rVert^2$
on $[-200,200]^3$ with a budget of $N=20$ runs. Each configuration (arm) is
run with 30 paired seeds, so the same seed gives every arm the same initial
design. The root mean square error (RMSE) is evaluated on one fixed set of
5000 test points, and arms are compared by a Wilcoxon signed-rank test on
$\log$ RMSE. A trial counts as a success if RMSE $<200$, an order of magnitude
below the value of about 2000 reached by a prior-reverted GP.

\subsection{Failure criterion}
A sweep over domain half-widths $h\in\{2,10,50,100,200\}$ shows a sharp
crossover between $h=2$, where the ratio of adaptive to passive-GP RMSE is
$\rho=1.06$, and $h=10$, where $\rho=0.38$ (Fig.~\ref{fig:dom}). The Schwefel
function on the larger domain $[-500,500]^3$, but with a small dynamic range,
gives $\rho=1.09$: passive GP sampling is adequate there. Across nine standard
benchmarks, only the problem that meets both conditions favours the adaptive
scheme; on seven of the nine, a well-specified passive GP ties or wins by
\qty{9}{\percent} to \qty{17}{\percent}.

Three findings follow. \emph{First}, the spectral metric gives no benefit:
plain variance succeeds in all 30 trials and has the lower paired RMSE in 18
of 30 ($p=0.047$). \emph{Second}, success depends on an \emph{anchor} member.
Without the PCE members the scheme fails in every trial, as does the
conventional adaptive design with a single GP. With them, hard rejection ends
with the degree-2 PCE alone in 26 of 30 trials; that PCE is the exact model
class of the dominant quadratic term, which carries more than \qty{99.9}{\percent} of the
output variance. \emph{Third}, hard rejection collapses to one member in all
30 trials, at a median of iteration 10 of 15. After that the disagreement is
identically zero, and about \qty{31}{\percent} of its run budget is silently spent on
random points.

Table~\ref{tab:agg} isolates the aggregation rule. With three survivors that
include the correct member, a uniform mean fails in every trial, because the
error of the prior-reverted GP, of order $10^3$, dominates it. The same three
members weighted by Eq.~\eqref{eq:w} succeed in every trial. Making rejection
reversible has no measurable effect: the aggregation rule is the causal
ingredient.

\subsection{Potential applications}
The results suggest four practical rules for using a surrogate inside a
GUM~S1 evaluation of an expensive model, such as a dispersion simulation in
a risk assessment.

\emph{Check the two failure conditions before trusting a GP.} If the input
ranges are wide compared with the spacing of the available runs, and the
output spans orders of magnitude, a passively sampled GP reproduces its prior
mean over most of the domain, and its own predictive variance does not reveal
this. Where either condition is absent, a GP fitted to a space-filling design
was as good as any adaptive scheme in our tests.

\emph{Report the surrogate as an uncertainty component.} Eq.~\eqref{eq:lotv}
gives a variance share for the surrogate that can be entered in the
uncertainty budget alongside the input contributions. A single surrogate, or a
committee reduced to one member, sets this share to zero by construction; that
is an omission, not a demonstration of accuracy.

\emph{Weight members by out-of-sample error, never uniformly.} One
prior-reverted member is enough to move a uniform mean by orders of magnitude,
even when an exact member is present. Cross-validation error is cheap at
$N\approx20$ and separates good and bad members by orders of magnitude, which
is all Eq.~\eqref{eq:w} needs.

\emph{Treat committee envelopes as lower bounds on model-form uncertainty.}
The exceedance intervals in Table~\ref{tab:uq} contain the reference value in
only \qty{62}{\percent} of seeds. They show \emph{that} the surrogate is uncertain, not with
what probability. Calibrated coverage needs a further step, such as conformal
prediction~\cite{angelopoulos2023}.

\section{Conclusions}
For an expensive measurement model evaluated about twenty times, a passively
sampled GP surrogate is safe for Monte Carlo propagation unless the input
domain is large and the output dynamic range is high. When both hold, a
heterogeneous committee restores accuracy, but the reason is a member matching
the dominant response structure, combined with weighting by
cross-validation error. A sophisticated run-selection metric is not
responsible, and uniform averaging fails even with the right member present.
The weighted committee also makes the surrogate's own model-form contribution
visible in the uncertainty budget, as a p-box and a variance share. These
results come from a single synthetic model in which an anchor member is
available. The final paper will add test cases with no such member and a
dispersion-type model, and will replace the uncalibrated envelopes with
conformal intervals of guaranteed coverage. Code and every per-trial record
are openly available at
\url{https://github.com/Yugam2508/fyp-active-learning-cfd}.
