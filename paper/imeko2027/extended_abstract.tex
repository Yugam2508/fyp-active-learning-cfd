\title{Surrogates for Monte Carlo uncertainty propagation with twenty model
runs}

\begin{abstract}
Monte Carlo propagation of distributions needs far more evaluations of the
measurement model than an expensive simulation allows, so the model is
replaced by a surrogate trained on about twenty runs. We compare surrogate
strategies on four test models, including a Gaussian plume model of an
ammonia release, over 30 paired repetitions, and score the propagated mean,
95th percentile and exceedance probability against a reference Monte Carlo
evaluation. The kernel specification of a Gaussian process changed the error
up to eighteen-fold; one missing hyperparameter produced an apparent failure
that an earlier analysis had attributed to domain size. Logarithmic
transformation of positive variables reduced the error up to eightfold.
Committees of different surrogates beat a well-specified Gaussian process
only where one member matched the response structure, and only with
cross-validation error weighting. Adaptive selection of runs did not help
consistently. We derive practical rules from these results.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Evaluating measurement uncertainty by the propagation of distributions,
following Supplement~1 to the Guide to the Expression of Uncertainty in
Measurement (GUM~S1)~\cite{jcgm100,jcgm101}, requires the measurement model
$Y=f(\mathbf{X})$ to be evaluated once per Monte Carlo trial, typically
$10^5$ to $10^6$ times~\cite{cox2006,harris2014}. For many quantities of
practical interest, $f$ is itself a numerical simulation. One example is the
concentration downstream of an accidental ammonia release~\cite{tan2017}: a
single computational fluid dynamics run takes hours to days, and a practical
budget is often fewer than twenty runs.

The usual remedy is a \emph{surrogate} (emulator) trained on those runs and
used in place of $f$ inside the Monte Carlo loop~\cite{heidenreich2017,
wang2022,ohagan2006}. The surrogate's error then becomes a contribution to the
uncertainty budget~\cite{kennedy2001}. Gaussian process (GP)
regression~\cite{sacks1989,rw2006} is the standard surrogate; a common
guideline asks for about ten runs per input~\cite{loeppky2009}, which many
applications cannot afford, and results are known to depend on modelling
choices~\cite{chen2016}. Several alternatives have been proposed for small
budgets: weighted committees of different surrogates~\cite{goel2007,
viana2009}, adaptive selection of runs~\cite{gramacy2009} and, recently,
spectral measures of committee disagreement~\cite{lin2024}.

An earlier analysis of such a scheme (heterogeneous committee, spectral
acquisition, dynamic model management) reported that a passively sampled GP
fails catastrophically on large input domains and that the committee recovers
accuracy. This contribution tests which of those conclusions survive a
controlled comparison with correctly specified baselines, on models chosen to
include cases where no committee member matches the response, and scored on
the quantities a GUM~S1 evaluation reports.

\section{Proposed contribution}
\subsection{Test models}
Four models with uniform input distributions are used (Table~\ref{tab:models}).
\emph{Physics} is the benchmark of the earlier analysis; its dominant
quadratic term lies exactly in the class of one committee member.
\emph{Plume} is the ground-level concentration of a continuous ammonia
release from a Gaussian plume model with Briggs rural dispersion coefficients
for neutral stability and ground reflection. Its output spans three orders of
magnitude and has a \qty{12}{\percent} probability of exceeding the level
immediately dangerous to life or health (IDLH) for ammonia,
\qty{210}{\milli\gram\per\cubic\metre} (about \num{300} ppm). \emph{Borehole}
(water flow) and \emph{Ishigami} are standard test functions for computer
experiments. No committee member matches the last three.

\subsection{Strategies}
Every strategy uses $N=20$ runs of the model. The single-GP strategies use a
20-point Latin hypercube with five kernel specifications: the original kernel
of the earlier analysis (squared exponential with one length scale and
\emph{no} amplitude hyperparameter), with an amplitude added, with one length
scale per input (automatic relevance determination, ARD), with both, and a
Mat\'ern-5/2 ARD kernel with amplitude. A further variant fits the latter to
the logarithm of the output, with logarithmic transformation of inputs whose
range is strictly positive (plume and borehole only). The adaptive GP starts
from five Latin hypercube runs and adds 15 at its largest posterior standard
deviation~\cite{gramacy2009}.

The committee has nine members: two GPs (with amplitude and ARD), polynomial
chaos expansions (PCE)~\cite{xiu2002,sudret2008} of degrees 2 and 3, two
random forests, a support vector regressor and two neural networks. Its
members are combined either uniformly or with weights
\begin{equation}
w_k\propto\left[\mathrm{MSE}_{\mathrm{CV},k}\,(1+\pi_k)\right]^{-1},
\label{eq:w}
\end{equation}
where $\mathrm{MSE}_{\mathrm{CV},k}$ is the three-fold cross-validation error
of member $k$ and $\pi_k\ge0$ penalises predictions below a known physical
bound~\cite{goel2007,viana2009}. The committee is trained either on the
20-point design (passive) or on five runs plus 15 chosen where its weighted
members disagree most, scored by their variance or by the spectral measure
UEigV~\cite{lin2024} (adaptive).

\subsection{Evaluation}
Each strategy is run with 30 seeds. Seeds are shared, so passive strategies
use the identical design and adaptive strategies the identical start. The
trained surrogate propagates \num{3e4} input draws, and the propagated mean,
standard deviation (SD), 95th percentile $y_{0.95}$ and exceedance probability
$P(Y>y_{0.95})$ are compared with a reference Monte Carlo evaluation of the
true model ($M=\num{2e5}$). Accuracy over the input domain is the root mean
square error (RMSE) on 5000 test points divided by the output SD (normalised
RMSE; 1 corresponds to a constant predictor). Strategies are compared by a
two-sided Wilcoxon signed-rank test on paired $\log$ normalised RMSE. Code and
every per-seed record are openly available~\cite{repo}.

\section{Preliminary results and potential applications}
\subsection{Kernel specification and variable transformation}
Table~\ref{tab:kernel} shows that the specification of a single GP matters
more than any other choice examined. On the physics model the original kernel
reaches 0.59, close to a constant predictor; adding the amplitude
hyperparameter alone reduces this to 0.033 (all 30 seeds improve,
$p<10^{-8}$). The catastrophic GP failure reported earlier is therefore a
specification artefact (Fig.~\ref{fig:dom}): with the original kernel the
normalised RMSE is 0.60 at every domain half-width from 10 to 200, whereas a
Mat\'ern-5/2 ARD GP with amplitude reaches 0.17 at 10 and 0.08 from 50 to 200.
The committee remains far more accurate on this model, but because of its
degree-2 PCE member, not because the GP fails. On the borehole model ARD matters (0.30 to 0.09).
Logarithmic transformation then gives the largest single improvement: from
0.089 to 0.011 on the borehole model and from 0.60 to 0.42 on the plume (30
and 27 of 30 seeds improve). No GP does better than 0.9 on the Ishigami
function, for which the ten-runs-per-input guideline~\cite{loeppky2009} would
ask for 30 runs.

\subsection{Committees and adaptive runs}
Fig.~\ref{fig:strategies} compares all strategies. A committee outperforms
the best single GP only on the physics model, by more than two orders of
magnitude, and only because the degree-2 PCE represents its dominant term exactly; the
weights then concentrate on that member. On the borehole model the
CV-weighted committee is 2.3 times worse than the Mat\'ern-5/2 GP on the same
design (0 of 30 seeds better), and on the plume and Ishigami models the two
tie. Aggregation matters wherever a committee helps: uniform averaging is
worse than CV weighting on the physics (by a factor of about 2000), plume and
borehole models ($p\le0.023$) and never better. Adaptive selection of runs
gave no consistent benefit. For the single GP it was never better and was
worse on the plume ($p=0.008$). For the committee it helped on the physics
and borehole models (median ratios 0.80 and 0.61), hurt on the plume (1.25)
and made no difference on the Ishigami function. Spectral (UEigV)
acquisition was worse than variance acquisition on three models (on the
borehole model by a factor of 1.7, $p<10^{-7}$) and better only on the plume
(0.90, $p=0.009$).

\subsection{Propagated quantities}
Table~\ref{tab:prop} gives the quantities a GUM~S1 evaluation reports, and
they follow the normalised RMSE. With the best strategy for each model, the
95th percentile is within \qty{0.41}{\percent} (physics), \qty{6.9}{\percent}
(plume), \qty{0.60}{\percent} (borehole) and \qty{16}{\percent} (Ishigami) of
the reference, and $P(Y>y_{0.95})$ within 0.0015, 0.0076, 0.0018 and 0.029
of 0.05. Fig.~\ref{fig:plume} shows the plume case for the first seed:
with log variables the GP reproduces the reference distribution over three
orders of magnitude and gives
$P(C>\qty{210}{\milli\gram\per\cubic\metre})=0.119$ against a reference
0.123, compared with 0.158 for the GP on raw variables and 0.142 for the
committee. Adaptive runs can mislead: the adaptive committee estimated the
plume mean with a median error of \qty{55}{\percent}. The member envelope of
a committee, read as a model-form interval for $P(Y>y_{0.95})$, contained
the reference value in between \qty{0}{\percent} and \qty{100}{\percent} of
seeds depending on model and strategy; it cannot replace a calibrated
interval~\cite{ferson1996,angelopoulos2023}.

\subsection{Potential applications}
The results suggest rules for surrogate-based GUM~S1 evaluations with a
budget of about twenty runs, such as a dispersion study in a risk assessment.
\emph{Specify the GP fully}: an amplitude hyperparameter and one length scale
per input; check the cross-validation error against the output SD before
propagating. \emph{Transform the variables} when the model is multiplicative
or positive, for example concentrations and flows. \emph{Use a committee only
with error weighting}, and expect a gain only when a member can represent the
dominant structure; otherwise a well-specified GP is as good or better.
\emph{Do not assume that adaptive selection of runs helps} at this budget;
a space-filling design is a safe default. \emph{Report the surrogate's error}
as a component of the uncertainty budget, estimated from held-out or
cross-validation error rather than from the spread of committee members.

\section{Conclusions}
For Monte Carlo uncertainty propagation with twenty runs of an expensive
model, the kernel specification of a GP surrogate and the transformation of
the variables mattered more than the choice between single surrogates,
committees and adaptive designs. A failure of GP surrogates reported in an
earlier analysis was caused by one missing hyperparameter. Committees helped
only when one member matched the response structure and only with
cross-validation error weighting, and adaptive selection of runs was not
reliably beneficial. The final paper will add a CFD-based dispersion case and
calibrated (conformal) intervals for the surrogate contribution.
