\title{Error-weighted surrogate committees for Monte Carlo propagation through
expensive models}

\begin{abstract}
Monte Carlo propagation of distributions needs thousands of evaluations of the
measurement model, which is infeasible when each evaluation is a fluid
dynamics simulation lasting hours. A surrogate trained on about twenty runs
makes propagation cheap but adds a rarely evaluated model-form uncertainty
component. On a benchmark with a controllable domain, a passively
sampled Gaussian process becomes no better than a constant predictor once the
run spacing is large. A committee of structurally different surrogates
recovers accuracy only when one member represents the dominant response
component; paired 30-seed ablations show that the run-selection metric does
not matter. Aggregation is decisive: uniform averaging failed in all 30
trials, while cross-validation error weighting succeeded whenever the
matching member was retained. The weighted committee reproduced a reference
Monte Carlo evaluation to within \qty{0.7}{\percent} and supplies a
model-form variance share and probability-box bounds for the uncertainty
budget.
\end{abstract}

\begin{keywords}
Measurement uncertainty; Monte Carlo method; Surrogate model; Active learning;
Model-form uncertainty
\end{keywords}

\section{Introduction}
Evaluating measurement uncertainty by the propagation of distributions,
following Supplement~1 to the Guide to the Expression of Uncertainty in
Measurement (GUM~S1)~\cite{jcgm100,jcgm101}, requires the measurement model
$Y=f(\mathbf{X})$ to be evaluated once per Monte Carlo trial, typically
$10^5$ to $10^6$ times~\cite{cox2006,harris2014}. For many
quantities of practical interest, $f$ is itself a numerical simulation. One
example is the concentration downstream of an accidental ammonia release in an
industrial hall~\cite{tan2017}. A single computational fluid dynamics (CFD) run
of such a release takes hours to days, while the standard error of a Monte
Carlo estimate falls only as $M^{-1/2}$ in the number of trials $M$. A
practical budget is often fewer than twenty runs.

The usual remedy is a \emph{surrogate} (emulator) trained on those runs and
used in place of $f$ inside the Monte Carlo loop~\cite{heidenreich2017,
wang2022,ohagan2006}. This trades an intractable cost for a new contribution to the
uncertainty budget: the error of the surrogate itself. From a metrological
point of view, this contribution must be evaluated and reported, not assumed
to be negligible. The standard surrogate, Gaussian process (GP)
regression~\cite{sacks1989,rw2006}, provides a predictive variance, but that
variance is conditional on the GP being an adequate model. Where it is not,
the GP can be confidently wrong~\cite{huellermeier2021}; such model
inadequacy has to be treated as an uncertainty source in its own
right~\cite{kennedy2001}.

Adaptive designs place new runs where a surrogate is most
uncertain~\cite{gramacy2009}. Committee-based schemes extend this idea by
training several structurally different surrogates, choosing new runs where
they disagree, and combining their predictions. Weighted combinations of surrogates are established in design
optimisation~\cite{goel2007,viana2009}, and spectral measures of disagreement
have recently been proposed for uncertainty quantification of language
models~\cite{lin2024}. Which ingredients of such schemes matter when they are
used for uncertainty \emph{evaluation} of an expensive measurement model has
not been isolated.

This contribution addresses three questions. When does a passively sampled
GP fail as a stand-in for an expensive model? When it fails, which
ingredients of an adaptive multi-surrogate scheme restore an accurate
propagation? And what does the scheme contribute to the uncertainty budget
beyond a central estimate?

\section{Proposed contribution}
\subsection{Prior reversion of passive GP surrogates}
For a GP with prior mean $\mu$, the posterior mean at an input $\mathbf{x}$ is
\begin{equation}
\hat f(\mathbf{x})=\mu+\mathbf{k}_\ast^{\top}
\left(\mathbf{K}+\sigma_\varepsilon^2\mathbf{I}\right)^{-1}
(\mathbf{y}-\mu\mathbf{1}),
\label{eq:gp}
\end{equation}
where $\mathbf{K}$ is the covariance matrix of the training inputs,
$\mathbf{k}_\ast$ the covariance between $\mathbf{x}$ and the training inputs,
$\mathbf{y}$ the training outputs and $\sigma_\varepsilon^2$ the noise
variance. When the distance from $\mathbf{x}$ to every training point greatly
exceeds the kernel length scale, $\mathbf{k}_\ast\to\mathbf{0}$ and
$\hat f\to\mu$: the GP reverts to a constant. We quantify when this happens
with a domain-size sweep, and ask whether, and why, an adaptive committee
avoids it.

\subsection{Committee scheme under study}
A committee of $K=9$ surrogates with different inductive biases is used: two
GPs, polynomial chaos expansions (PCE)~\cite{xiu2002,sudret2008} of degrees
2 and 3, two random forests,
a support vector regressor and two neural networks. The committee is trained on
a five-point Latin hypercube design, and fifteen further runs are then chosen
sequentially where the members disagree most (Algorithm~\ref{alg:al}).
Disagreement is scored either by a spectral measure (UEigV, the eigenvalue
sum of the normalised graph Laplacian built from the $K$
predictions~\cite{lin2024}) or by the plain committee variance.

Two \emph{model managers} combine the members. Hard rejection removes members
that are inconsistent with the committee and averages the survivors uniformly.
Error weighting assigns
\begin{equation}
w_k\propto\left[\mathrm{MSE}_{\mathrm{CV},k}\,(1+\pi_k)\right]^{-1},
\label{eq:w}
\end{equation}
where $\mathrm{MSE}_{\mathrm{CV},k}$ is the cross-validation mean squared
error of member $k$ and $\pi_k\ge0$ optionally penalises violations of known
physical bounds, such as a non-negative concentration. Eq.~\eqref{eq:w} is the
weighted-average surrogate of~\cite{goel2007,viana2009}. We do not propose it
as new; we test when it is \emph{necessary}.

\begin{algorithm}[tb]
\caption{Adaptive committee surrogate for propagation}
\label{alg:al}
\small
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require input PDF $g_{\mathbf X}$, budget $N$, $N_0=5$, acquisition $a$
\State $\mathcal D\gets$ Latin hypercube of $N_0$ runs of $f$; fit all members
\For{$t=1,\dots,N-N_0$}
  \State $\mathbf x^\ast\gets\arg\max a(\mathbf x)$ over a random candidate pool
  \State run $f(\mathbf x^\ast)$, add it to $\mathcal D$, refit the members
  \State update weights by Eq.~\eqref{eq:w}, or reject members
\EndFor
\State draw $\mathbf X_m\sim g_{\mathbf X}$, $m=1,\dots,M$; evaluate all members
\State \Return $\mu_w$, $\sigma^2_{\mathrm{mf}}$, p-box, exceedance bounds
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\subsection{Propagation with a model-form component}
The trained committee replaces $f$ in a GUM~S1 Monte Carlo evaluation, here
with $M=\num{3e4}$ trials, which takes seconds of computing time. By the law
of total variance,
\begin{equation}
\operatorname{Var}[Y]=\operatorname{Var}_{\mathbf{X}}\left[\mu_w(\mathbf{X})\right]
+\mathrm{E}_{\mathbf{X}}\left[\sigma^2_{\mathrm{mf}}(\mathbf{X})\right],
\label{eq:lotv}
\end{equation}
the output variance splits into an input-driven part, carried by the weighted
committee mean $\mu_w$, and a model-form part $\sigma^2_{\mathrm{mf}}$, the
weighted spread between members. The second term's share of the total is a
stopping diagnostic: further simulator runs are worthwhile while it is large.
Independently of the weights, the members' output cumulative distribution
functions (CDFs) span a probability box (p-box)~\cite{ferson1996}, from which
an exceedance probability $P(Y>y_{\mathrm c})$ is reported as an interval.

\section{Preliminary results and potential applications}
\subsection{Protocol}
The test model is
$f(\mathbf{x})=\sin 2x_1\cos 2x_2+\mathrm{e}^{-x_3^2}+0.2\lVert\mathbf{x}\rVert^2$,
observed with noise of standard deviation 0.1, on $[-h,h]^3$ with $h=200$
unless stated. The budget is $N=20$ runs. Each configuration (arm) is run with
30 seeds shared by all arms. The root mean square error (RMSE) is evaluated on
one fixed set of 5000 test points, and arms are compared by a two-sided
Wilcoxon signed-rank test on $\log$ RMSE. A trial counts as a success if
RMSE $<200$; at $h=200$ the output standard deviation (SD) is about 4100 and a
prior-reverted GP reaches about 2400, so successes and failures differ by
more than two orders of magnitude (Fig.~\ref{fig:strip}). Code, per-seed
records and the scripts that produce every number and figure are openly
available~\cite{repo}.

\subsection{When the passive GP fails}
Fig.~\ref{fig:dom} compares a GP fitted to a 20-point Latin hypercube with the
adaptive committee (UEigV, hard rejection) as the half-width $h$ grows. The
passive GP's median RMSE is 1.00 output SD at $h=2$ and 0.60 SD for every
$h\ge10$: it reproduces little more than the mean. The committee's error falls
from 0.99 SD at $h=2$ to \num{1.7e-4} SD at $h=200$, and the ratio of mean
RMSEs from 1.04 to 0.030. The mechanism is visible in absolute terms. The
quadratic term carries \qty{44}{\percent} of the output variance at $h=2$ and
practically all of it for $h\ge10$, and the degree-2 PCE represents it
exactly. The committee's median RMSE is 0.62 to 0.68 at $h=2$ and
$h\ge50$, close to the RMS of the oscillatory and exponential terms (0.50 to
0.61), which no member can learn from 20 runs. At $h=10$ the median is 1.99,
because hard rejection retains the degree-2 PCE in only 15 of 30 trials. On
the Schwefel function on $[-500,500]^3$, which no member represents, both
methods stay at about one SD (ratio 1.10). A passive GP therefore fails
whenever the run spacing is large compared with the length scale of the
response, and a committee helps only to the extent that one member represents
the dominant component.

Three findings follow. \emph{First}, the spectral metric gives no benefit:
plain variance succeeds in all 30 trials and has the lower paired RMSE in 18
of 30 ($p=0.047$). \emph{Second}, success depends on an \emph{anchor} member.
Without the PCE members the scheme fails in every trial, as does the
conventional adaptive design with a single GP. With them, hard rejection ends
with the degree-2 PCE alone in 27 of 30 trials. \emph{Third}, hard rejection
collapses the committee to one member in all 30 trials, at a median of
iteration 10 of 15. After that the disagreement is identically zero,
and \qty{31}{\percent} of the run budget is silently spent on random points.

Table~\ref{tab:agg} isolates the aggregation rule. With $K_{\min}=3$ the
degree-2 PCE survives in 27 of 30 trials, yet the uniform mean fails in all
30, because the error of the prior-reverted members, of order $10^3$,
dominates it. Weighting the same survivors on the same data by
Eq.~\eqref{eq:w} succeeds in all 27 trials that retain the degree-2 PCE and
fails in exactly the three that do not (paired $p<10^{-8}$). The aggregation
rule, not the reversibility of rejection, is the causal ingredient, and it can
only exploit an anchor that is present.

For a representative run (seed 1000, the first seed), the weighted committee
matches the reference mean to \qty{0.21}{\percent}, the SD to
\qty{0.14}{\percent} and the 5th, 50th and 95th percentiles to
\qty{0.66}{\percent}, \qty{0.24}{\percent} and \qty{0.41}{\percent}
(Fig.~\ref{fig:pbox}). Its model-form share in Eq.~\eqref{eq:lotv} falls from
0.50 at five runs to \num{7e-8} at twenty, as the weights concentrate on the
degree-2 PCE. This is consistent with the actual surrogate error, whose share
of the output variance is $\mathrm{RMSE}^2/\operatorname{Var}[Y]\approx
\num{2e-8}$. The p-box and exceedance interval are unweighted member
envelopes. They remain wide and are not calibrated coverage intervals: the
exceedance interval contains the reference value in only
\qty{62}{\percent} of seeds. A committee reduced to one member by hard
rejection gives zero-width intervals and a model-form share of exactly zero
by construction, so it cannot report this component at all.

\subsection{Potential applications}
The results suggest four practical rules for using a surrogate inside a
GUM~S1 evaluation of an expensive model, such as a dispersion simulation in
a risk assessment.

\emph{Check for prior reversion before trusting a GP.} If the input ranges are
wide compared with the spacing of the available runs, a passively sampled GP
reproduces little more than its mean, and its own predictive variance does
not reveal this. Comparing its cross-validation error with the output SD is a
cheap check.

\emph{Include members that can represent the expected structure.} A committee
helps only through a member whose hypothesis class matches the dominant
response component, for example a low-order polynomial for a smooth trend.
The choice of run-selection metric is secondary.

\emph{Weight members by out-of-sample error, never uniformly.} One
prior-reverted member is enough to move a uniform mean by orders of magnitude,
even when an exact member is present. Cross-validation error is cheap at
$N\approx20$ and separates good and bad members by orders of magnitude.

\emph{Report the model-form component, and treat envelopes as indicative.}
The weighted share in Eq.~\eqref{eq:lotv} can be entered in the uncertainty
budget alongside the input contributions; a single surrogate sets it to zero
by construction, which is an omission rather than evidence of accuracy.
Member envelopes show \emph{that} the surrogate is uncertain, not with what
probability; calibrated coverage needs a further step, such as conformal
prediction~\cite{angelopoulos2023}.

\section{Conclusions}
For an expensive measurement model evaluated about twenty times, a passively
sampled GP surrogate reverts towards a constant once the run spacing is large
compared with the length scale of the response. A heterogeneous committee
restores accuracy only through a member that represents the dominant response
component, and only if the members are combined by out-of-sample error
weighting: uniform averaging failed in every trial even with that member
present, and the choice of run-selection metric made no difference. The
weighted committee reproduces a reference Monte Carlo evaluation to better
than \qty{1}{\percent} and makes the surrogate's own contribution visible in
the uncertainty budget. These results come from a synthetic model in which an
anchor member is available. The final paper will add test cases with no such
member and a dispersion-type model, and will replace the uncalibrated
envelopes with conformal intervals.
