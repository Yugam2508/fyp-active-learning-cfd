\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}
For GUM~S1 evaluations in which the measurement model can be run only a few
tens of times, the way a surrogate is specified matters more than the
strategy around it. A GP should estimate its amplitude and one length scale
per input and work on the log scale when the output is positive; a failure
of GP surrogates reported in an earlier analysis was an artefact of one
hyperparameter held fixed. A quadratic trend helps when the response has
one but can overfit when there are many inputs, so it belongs among the
candidates rather than in every model. Among such candidates and a sparse
PCE, the one with the smallest leave-one-out error
was practically as good as the best in hindsight on all twelve models from
20 runs on,
including a CFD simulation of a release behind a building. Committees and
stacking of surrogates gained little or nothing, the type of space-filling
design hardly mattered, and variance-driven adaptive designs gave no
consistent gain at 20 runs,
whereas a design targeted at the threshold improved exceedance
probabilities when the threshold lay in a tail of the distribution. Finally, the surrogate's own error must be part of the
uncertainty statement, and it should be estimated from leave-one-out
residuals rather than from the GP's predictive variance: the former
restored the nominal coverage of the GUM~S1 coverage interval, and the
jackknife over the leave-one-out fits gave intervals for reported
quantities that reached it in 39 of 42 cases with 20 runs; the latter was
overconfident precisely where the surrogate was poor, and fell short also
with its hyperparameters integrated out. The contribution held from 10 to
80 runs, with other space-filling designs, with dependent inputs and for
vector outputs (with the residuals of all components resampled together).
Its limit is that of any estimate from the runs: a
local feature that no run reaches is not seen, and where one is plausible,
prior knowledge or runs placed there are needed.
