When the measurement model is an expensive simulation, Monte Carlo
propagation of distributions according to GUM Supplement~1 is carried out
on a surrogate fitted to a few tens of model runs, and the surrogate's error
becomes part of the uncertainty budget. We compare Gaussian processes (GPs)
of different specifications, sparse polynomial chaos expansions, committees
of surrogates, and space-filling, adaptive and threshold-targeted designs on
twelve models with up to 20 inputs, including GUM-type input distributions and a computational
fluid dynamics (CFD) simulation of a gas release behind a building, with
10 to 80 runs and 30 paired repetitions against reference Monte Carlo
evaluations. The specification of the surrogate mattered more than the
choice of strategy: an estimated kernel amplitude, a quadratic trend and
the log scale for positive quantities reduced the error by factors of up
to 18, 400 and 9, and choosing among such candidates by their
leave-one-out error recovered practically all of the gain without access
to the true model. Committees did not beat a GP with a trend, and
variance-driven adaptive designs gave no consistent gain at 20 runs and
helped only with larger budgets on smooth models, whereas a design targeted
at a threshold in the tail reduced the error of exceedance probabilities up
to ninefold. Adding the leave-one-out
residuals of the surrogate to every Monte Carlo trial brought the achieved
coverage of the GUM~S1 95\% coverage interval to 91--97\% on all models,
and jackknife+ prediction intervals covered 95--100\% of test points,
whereas the GP's own predictive intervals covered as few as 68\%, and its
intervals for a 95th percentile contained the true value in a median of
only 63\% of repetitions. The results are condensed into a validated
procedure for surrogate-based uncertainty evaluation.
